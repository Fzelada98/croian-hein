Pétrus 1946, and what a post-war bottle is.

September 20263 minCroian & Hein

In 1946 Pétrus was not the most expensive wine in the world. It was a small Pomerol property with good clay and a determined owner, and almost nobody outside Bordeaux had heard of it.

  2. What 1946 actually was
  3. How to think about the bottle

Write to Bárbara

The interest of a 1946 Pétrus is not that 1946 was a great vintage. It was not. The interest is that the bottle exists at all, from a year when the vineyard had barely been reclaimed, the men who worked it were still coming home, and the estate was a respected local name rather than a global one.

The place

Pétrus is about eleven hectares on the highest part of the Pomerol plateau, on a button of blue clay that appears nowhere else in quite the same form. The wine is made almost entirely from Merlot. There is no château in any meaningful sense: the buildings are modest and always were. The soil did the work.

Pomerol has no classification. Nothing was ranked in 1855 and nothing has been ranked since, which is part of why the best properties on the right bank took so much longer to be recognised than their equivalents in the Médoc, and part of why Le Pin, a few hectares away, could appear in 1979 and become one of the most expensive wines in the world within a decade.

What 1946 actually was

A small, difficult, post-war crop. The vineyards of Bordeaux had been under-worked for six years, materials were scarce, and the labour had not returned. Very little wine was made anywhere and less was made to keep.

1947 is the celebrated right-bank vintage of that period, hot and extraordinary. 1945 is the celebrated year before it. 1946 sits between two legends and is, by vintage reputation, the least of the three. A bottle of it is bought for what it is rather than for what the year promised: a survivor from a year almost nobody laid down, from an estate that had not yet become what it became.

How to think about the bottle

Everything about an eighty-year-old bottle is a condition question, and the vintage chart is the least useful document available.

  • The level. The 1946 here is a very top-shoulder fill, and the 1954 beside it is the

same. At eighty years old, that is a genuinely fine level, and what it records is that the bottle has lived somewhere cold and stable. The note on fill levels sets out the scale.

  • The provenance. Pétrus is among the most counterfeited names in the world, for the obvious reason. The

ten questions apply here more than anywhere.

  • The quantity. One bottle. Not a case with eleven others to compare it against, which changes how it is

priced and how it should be bought. See the note on single bottles.

  • The rest. A bottle of this age needs a month standing upright before it is opened, and a decision about

whether it is going to be opened at all. The transport note covers the first part; the second is yours.

The rest of the Pétrus here

The 1946 and the 1954 are the two oldest. Behind them the cellar runs through 1969, 1971, 1973, 1974, 1976, the 1982 in two bottles at superb levels, a 1985 magnum, 1986, 1995, 2003, 2012, 2013, 2017, 2020, and a 2024 bought en primeur.

Read as a sequence it is a reasonable history of the estate: the thin post-war years, the uneven 1970s, the 1982 that changed how Bordeaux was written about, and a modern run bought steadily by someone who was not trying to assemble a trophy shelf.

Questions we are asked
Was 1946 a good vintage in Bordeaux?

No. It was a small, difficult post-war crop from vineyards that had been under-worked for six years, and it sits between the celebrated 1945 and 1947. Bottles are valued for rarity and survival rather than for the reputation of the year.

What makes Pétrus different from other Bordeaux estates?

About eleven hectares on a button of blue clay at the highest point of the Pomerol plateau, planted almost entirely to Merlot, with no château to speak of. Pomerol has no classification of any kind.

What fill level should an eighty-year-old bottle have?

Upper shoulder is a fine level at that age and mid shoulder is common. A very top-shoulder fill on a 1946 is exceptional and indicates cold, stable storage throughout its life.

How long should a 1946 bottle rest before opening?

About a month standing upright after any journey, so the sediment settles completely. Opening an old bottle soon after transport pours the sediment into the glass.
